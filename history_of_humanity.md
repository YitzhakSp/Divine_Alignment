## 1. The Original Consciousness of Transcendence

- **The original intuition.** In ancient times, humanity possessed a deep intuitive awareness that human life and existence originated from a transcendent Source. This awareness was not necessarily the product of philosophical reasoning, but a fundamental dimension of human consciousness.
- **Different manifestations of the same intuition.** Across ancient civilizations, this awareness found expression in diverse religious traditions and practices. Some preserved a relatively pure recognition of the ultimate Source, as exemplified by Judaism, while others expressed the same underlying intuition in distorted forms, such as the worship of natural forces, celestial bodies, and spiritual intermediaries.

## 2. Weakening of consciousness of the Source

- **The beginning of a profound transformation.** The destruction of the First Temple in Jerusalem (586 BCE) marked the beginning of a gradual weakening of humanity's original intuitive awareness of the transcendent Source. What had once been experienced as a fundamental dimension of existence increasingly became a matter of belief, philosophical speculation, or intellectual debate.
    
- **The emergence of philosophical reasoning.** Around the same broad historical period, philosophical traditions began developing, particularly in ancient Greece. Human reason increasingly emerged as an independent means of investigating reality and addressing questions previously approached primarily through religious consciousness.
    
- **Christianity and Islam: Two distortions of the understanding of God and humanity's relationship with Him.** While the emergence of Christianity and Islam demonstrated that humanity still retained some level of intuition of the transcendent Source, even after the destruction of the 2nd temple, it also was evidence of how much this connection was dimmed, since both religions introduced significant distortions in the understanding of God and humanity's relationship with Him. Christianity blurred the distinction between Creator and creation by attributing divinity to a human being, thereby placing man at the center of the divine-human relationship. Islam, in contrast, emphasized God's absolute sovereignty and submission to His will to such an extent that the role of human agency and the reciprocal relationship between God and humanity risked being diminished.
    
- **The culmination of humanity's disconnection from the Source.** The Enlightenment period that started in 18th century marked a decisive acceleration of the historical process of the weakening of humanity's recognition of the Source, as human reason increasingly asserted its independence from religious revelation and tradition. During the nineteenth century, scientific developments—particularly Darwin's theory of evolution—and the rise of philosophical materialism strengthened the conviction that reality could be explained without reference to a transcendent Source. Throughout the twentieth century, further scientific advances and widespread secularization reinforced this transformation. Consequently, for a substantial portion of contemporary Western civilization, God is no longer perceived as the self-evident foundation of existence.
