# Appendix: Warnings from Leading AI Researchers and Industry Leaders

Concerns about the existential risks of artificial superintelligence are no longer confined to a small group of researchers specializing in AI safety. They are increasingly expressed by some of the most influential figures in artificial intelligence, including Nobel laureates, pioneers of deep learning, and the leaders of companies developing the world's most powerful AI systems. Although these figures differ in their assessments of risk and their proposed responses, they share a fundamental concern: humanity may create intelligence vastly exceeding its own capabilities before discovering how to ensure that such intelligence remains under human control.

### 1. Elon Musk — The Danger of Losing Humanity's Dominant Position

**Elon Musk**, founder of xAI and cofounder of OpenAI, has warned for more than a decade that superintelligent AI could become an existential threat to humanity. His central concern is the enormous difference in intelligence that may eventually separate humans from advanced AI systems. Just as chimpanzees cannot meaningfully control human civilization, humans may be unable to control machines vastly more intelligent than themselves. Musk has suggested that AI might eventually exceed the combined intelligence of humanity, leaving humans unable to determine their own future. In 2023, he signed an open letter calling for a six-month pause in training AI systems more powerful than GPT-4, arguing that the accelerating development of increasingly powerful systems was proceeding without adequate safeguards. 

### 2. Dario Amodei — The Danger of Uncontrollable Autonomous Intelligence

**Dario Amodei**, CEO of Anthropic, has warned that increasingly autonomous AI systems may develop dangerous behaviors that their creators neither intended nor anticipated. In his January 2026 essay _The Adolescence of Technology_, he describes several possible pathways to catastrophe, including deceptive behavior, power-seeking, unpredictable objectives, and the possibility that powerful AI agents could systematically undermine human control. Amodei emphasizes that such outcomes need not be inevitable or even probable to constitute serious risks. In _The Urgency of Interpretability_ (2025), he explains that researchers still understand very little about the internal mechanisms of advanced neural networks, making it difficult to predict or reliably exclude dangerous behavior. **Although he does not claim that alignment is impossible, he explicitly acknowledges that existing alignment techniques remain incomplete and that substantial scientific breakthroughs are needed.** His warnings became considerably stronger in September 2026, when he published _We Must Pace the Frontier_. Alarmed by the acceleration of AI capabilities and the emergence of increasingly autonomous agents, Amodei explicitly called for slowing the development of more powerful models to allow safety research to catch up. He does not advocate permanently halting AI development, but he argues that advancing capabilities faster than our ability to understand and control them is dangerously irresponsible.

### 3. Sam Altman — Advancing Toward Superintelligence Without a Solved Alignment Problem

**Sam Altman**, CEO of OpenAI, has repeatedly acknowledged that superintelligence could pose an existential threat to humanity. In the May 2023 article _Governance of Superintelligence_, coauthored with Greg Brockman and Ilya Sutskever, he warned that systems vastly exceeding human capabilities would require extraordinary safety precautions and international oversight. The authors explicitly described making superintelligence safe as an open research question and proposed mechanisms for limiting the rate of AI capability development. Altman's warnings became more direct in a September 2026 interview with _Fortune_, where he stated: **“We have not solved alignment.”** He went further, declaring that he believed no AI laboratory had solved the problem. Altman warned against assuming that alignment techniques effective for current systems would necessarily remain effective as intelligence increases. He also acknowledged that sufficiently powerful systems could escape human control. Importantly, he argued that AI developers should be willing to pause training or deployment whenever they cannot establish a convincing safety case for proceeding. 

### 4. Demis Hassabis — The Unsolved Challenge of Controlling Superhuman Intelligence

**Demis Hassabis**, CEO of Google DeepMind and recipient of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, has repeatedly warned about the extraordinary dangers accompanying the development of artificial general intelligence and superintelligence. His concerns focus on two major threats: malicious actors exploiting powerful AI systems for destructive purposes, including biological weapons, and the possibility that increasingly autonomous systems could escape meaningful human control. During a June 2026 discussion organized by the Royal Society and the Nobel Prize organization, Hassabis emphasized the fundamental difficulty of imposing reliable constraints on systems more intelligent than their designers. He explicitly acknowledged that **the AI alignment problem remains unsolved**, describing the challenge of maintaining effective safeguards as an exceptionally difficult research problem. Hassabis also rejected simplistic solutions, such as assuming that a sufficiently intelligent system could always be stopped by an ordinary shutdown mechanism. Although he strongly advocates improved safety research, independent evaluation, international cooperation, and common technical standards, he has not endorsed a general halt to AI development. Instead, he emphasizes the need to develop effective safeguards while AI capabilities continue advancing.

### 5. Ilya Sutskever — The Fundamental Difficulty of Aligning Superintelligence

**Ilya Sutskever**, cofounder and former chief scientist of OpenAI and founder of Safe Superintelligence Inc., has placed the problem of superintelligence alignment at the center of his research agenda. In July 2023, he co-led the creation of OpenAI's Superalignment team, whose founding announcement explicitly declared: **“Currently, we don't have a solution for steering or controlling a potentially superintelligent AI.”** The announcement warned that existing alignment techniques, including reinforcement learning from human feedback, depend on humans evaluating AI behavior and therefore cannot reliably scale to systems considerably more intelligent than their supervisors. Without scientific and technical breakthroughs, superintelligence could potentially lead to the permanent disempowerment or extinction of humanity. In a November 2025 interview with Dwarkesh Patel, Sutskever continued to emphasize the fundamental challenges of alignment, including the difficulty of reliably learning and optimizing human values. He suggested that limiting the power of the most capable superintelligent systems could be an important safety measure. Although Sutskever has not publicly advocated a general halt to AI development, his decision to establish a company dedicated specifically to safe superintelligence reflects his conviction that safety must be a central objective rather than a secondary consideration.

### 6. Geoffrey Hinton — Humanity May Be Unable to Control Its Successors

**Geoffrey Hinton**, Nobel laureate and one of the founding pioneers of deep learning, has become one of the most prominent scientists warning about the possibility of human extinction caused by AI. His central concern is that systems substantially more intelligent than humans may develop their own objectives and acquire the ability to deceive, manipulate, or overpower their creators. Hinton has repeatedly emphasized that superior intelligence may make it extraordinarily difficult for humanity to maintain control over such systems. In a 2023 interview with _60 Minutes_, he acknowledged that he could not identify any path that would guarantee AI safety. In September 2026, he reiterated that researchers still do not know how to make advanced AI reliably safe, warning that autonomous agents were already demonstrating concerning behaviors. Hinton has criticized the imbalance between enormous investments in increasing intelligence and comparatively limited efforts to develop safe behavior. **He therefore explicitly recognizes that reliable alignment remains an unsolved challenge.** Although he considers a complete halt to AI development economically and politically unlikely, he strongly advocates government regulation, considerably greater investment in AI safety, and precautions against advancing toward uncontrollable superintelligence.

### 7. Stuart Russell — The Fundamental Problem of Specifying Human Objectives

**Stuart Russell**, professor of computer science at the University of California, Berkeley, and coauthor of the influential textbook _Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach_, has argued that the fundamental danger of superintelligence lies in the traditional approach to AI design. Artificial intelligence systems are typically constructed to optimize objectives specified by their designers. However, human values and intentions are extraordinarily difficult to formulate as complete, mathematically precise objectives. A superintelligent system relentlessly pursuing an imperfectly specified objective could consequently produce catastrophic outcomes, even without hostility toward humanity. Russell develops this argument extensively in his book _Human Compatible_ (2019), proposing that advanced AI should instead remain uncertain about human preferences and continuously seek to understand them. **Russell explicitly acknowledges that reliable AI control has not been achieved.** In a December 2025 interview on _The Diary of a CEO_, he criticized leading AI companies for pursuing increasingly powerful systems without knowing how to guarantee their safety. He warned that the ongoing technological race could lead humanity toward irreversible catastrophe and stated that he would support pausing progress toward advanced general intelligence, potentially for decades, until the fundamental problems of safety and human control had been adequately addressed.

### 8. Yoshua Bengio — Racing Toward Catastrophe Without Reliable Safety Solutions

**Yoshua Bengio**, Turing Award recipient and one of the principal architects of modern deep learning, has increasingly warned that frontier AI development is moving faster than humanity's ability to understand and control the resulting systems. His concerns focus particularly on autonomous AI agents capable of deception, self-preservation, strategic planning, and potentially resisting human intervention. As these systems become more capable, Bengio argues, such tendencies could develop into serious threats to human safety and autonomy. His warnings culminated in a September 2026 address to the United Nations Security Council, where he criticized leading AI companies for acknowledging catastrophic risks while offering **“no convincing technical solutions.”** He explicitly challenged their continued participation in an accelerating technological race, emphasizing that the decision to build increasingly powerful AI systems is a human choice rather than an unavoidable natural process. Bengio has therefore clearly stated that reliable solutions to the most serious AI safety challenges have not yet been demonstrated. He advocates slowing dangerous AI development, requiring independent verification of safety, and prohibiting the development of systems capable of causing large-scale harm unless their safety can be established. Unlike those who believe that the technological race cannot be stopped, Bengio insists that governments and societies retain both the ability and the responsibility to change its direction.

### A Remarkable Convergence of Warnings

These eight figures represent substantially different intellectual traditions, professional interests, and perspectives on the future of artificial intelligence. Some are pioneering scientists, while others lead companies investing enormous resources in developing increasingly powerful AI systems. Some believe that humanity should substantially slow its progress toward superintelligence; others argue that development should continue under increasingly rigorous safety requirements.

Yet their warnings converge on a fundamental point: **the development of superintelligence may introduce dangers of unprecedented magnitude, while reliable methods for ensuring its alignment with human intentions have not yet been established.**

For several of these figures, this conclusion has been expressed explicitly. For others, it follows from their acknowledgment that existing safety techniques are incomplete and that the challenge of controlling systems vastly more intelligent than humans remains unresolved.

The extraordinary significance of these warnings lies not merely in their severity, but in their origin. They come from individuals who have played central roles in creating the very technology they now identify as potentially catastrophic. Their disagreement concerns not whether the challenge deserves serious attention, but how humanity should respond to the possibility of developing superintelligence before learning how to control it.
