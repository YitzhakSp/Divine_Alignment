# Humanity's Gradual Disconnection from the Source

## 1. The Original Consciousness of Transcendence

In ancient times, humanity possessed a deep intuitive awareness that human life and existence originated from a transcendent Source. This awareness was not necessarily the product of philosophical reasoning or intellectual investigation. Rather, it was a fundamental dimension of human consciousness—a recognition that existence itself depended upon a reality beyond the material world.

Across ancient civilizations, this intuition found expression in diverse religious traditions, beliefs, and practices. Yet these expressions differed profoundly in their understanding of the Source and humanity's relationship with it. Some preserved a relatively pure recognition of the ultimate Creator, as exemplified by Judaism. Others manifested the same underlying intuition in distorted forms, worshipping the sun, the stars, natural forces, or spiritual intermediaries. Idolatry, from this perspective, was not simply a false understanding of reality, but a distorted expression of a fundamentally true intuition: that human existence is rooted in something transcending humanity itself.

Despite their profound differences, these civilizations shared an essential characteristic: the recognition that the material world was not the ultimate foundation of existence. The transcendent Source remained a central reality in humanity's understanding of itself and the universe.

## 2. The Gradual Weakening of Consciousness of the Source

The destruction of the First Temple in Jerusalem in 586 BCE marked, from a Jewish spiritual perspective, the beginning of a profound transformation in humanity's relationship with the transcendent Source (ref_1). What had once been experienced as a fundamental dimension of existence began gradually to fade from human consciousness. Over the following centuries, the recognition of the Source increasingly became a matter of religious belief, philosophical speculation, or intellectual debate rather than immediate intuitive awareness.

It was no coincidence, that around this broad historical period (destruction of first temple), a profound intellectual transformation began unfolding. The emergence of philosophy in ancient Greece can be understood as an early manifestation of humanity's weakening intuitive connection with the transcendent Source. As the immediate awareness of the Source gradually faded, human beings increasingly turned to independent rational inquiry in search of answers to the most fundamental questions of existence: the origins of the universe, the nature of reality, and humanity's place within it. Philosophy did not necessarily reject transcendence; indeed, many philosophers continued to recognize a divine foundation of existence. Yet the very emergence of systematic philosophical inquiry into these ultimate questions can be seen as evidence of a profound shift: **what had once been intuitively recognized increasingly became something humanity sought to discover and understand through reason.**

The subsequent emergence of Christianity and Islam revealed another dimension of this historical process. On the one hand, the extraordinary spread of these two monotheistic religions demonstrated that humanity still retained a powerful intuition of the transcendent Source, even after the destruction of the Second Temple. On the other hand, from a Jewish theological perspective, both religions manifested significant distortions in their understanding of God and humanity's relationship with Him. Christianity blurred the fundamental distinction between Creator and creation by attributing divinity to a human being, thereby placing the human figure at the center of the divine-human relationship. Islam, in contrast, placed overwhelming emphasis on God's absolute sovereignty and humanity's submission to His will, potentially diminishing the significance of human agency and the reciprocal relationship between God and humanity. Although these are theological interpretations rather than descriptions universally accepted by the traditions themselves, they illustrate two contrasting departures from the understanding of God preserved in Judaism. Thus, the rise of these religions can be understood simultaneously as evidence of humanity's enduring recognition of the Source and of the diminishing clarity of that recognition.

The dynamics that was outlined above, accelerated dramatically with the European Enlightenment of the eighteenth century. Human reason increasingly asserted its independence from religious revelation and tradition, while the extraordinary achievements of modern science strengthened confidence in humanity's ability to understand reality through natural laws. During the nineteenth century, Darwin's theory of evolution and the growing influence of philosophical materialism reinforced the conviction that the natural world, including human life itself, could be explained without reference to a transcendent Creator. Throughout the twentieth century, further advances in physics, cosmology, biology, and neuroscience, together with the widespread secularization of Western societies, contributed to the consolidation of this intellectual orientation.

These developments did not logically disprove the existence of a transcendent Source, nor did they eliminate religious consciousness from Western civilization. Nevertheless, they profoundly transformed the prevailing intellectual outlook. For a substantial portion of contemporary Western society, God is no longer perceived as the self-evident foundation of existence. The question of the ultimate Source has increasingly been pushed to the margins of intellectual and cultural life, regarded as a matter of personal faith rather than a fundamental dimension of reality.

**Humanity has thus undergone a remarkable transformation: from a civilization in which the transcendent Source was intuitively recognized, even when misunderstood, to one in which the Source can be disregarded altogether.**
