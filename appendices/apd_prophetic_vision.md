# Introduction

Every day, at the end of each of the three daily services, Jews say a prayer called *Aleinu* (ref_1). Its first half thanks God for the special task given to Israel. Its second half looks at the whole world, and this is where the hope appears. Here are its main lines in translation:

> _"Therefore we put our hope in You, Lord our God, soon to see the glory of Your might, to remove idolatry from the earth, and the false gods will be utterly cut off; to perfect the world under the kingdom of the Almighty. All humankind will call upon Your name, to turn to You all the wicked of the earth. All the inhabitants of the world will recognize and know that to You every knee shall bend, every tongue shall swear."_

The prayer ends with a line from the Torah, _"The Lord will reign for ever and ever"_ (Exodus 15:18), and with a verse from Zechariah that we will meet in section 3 (the full text is in ref_1). Three things stand out. First, the hope is for everyone: *all* humankind will call on God's name, and *all* the inhabitants of the world will come to know Him. Second, it is about how people live and not only what they believe: the goal is "to perfect the world", and even "the wicked of the earth" are invited back. Third, it is said every day. **This is not a doctrine that lives only in books, but a hope renewed at the end of every service, as it has been through centuries of exile and persecution.**

This hope did not begin with the prayer. It comes from the prophets. **The vision of a world turned toward God is not tied to one verse or one prophet.** It appears in Isaiah, Jeremiah, Micah, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Zechariah, Obadiah and Psalms, written over several centuries in very different circumstances. That is why the essay speaks of a prophetic *horizon* and not of a single prediction: the texts do not repeat one sentence, they approach the same future from different directions.

This appendix gathers those texts under five themes. **(1) Knowledge of God fills the world. (2) The nations turn to God and come to learn from Him. (3) God's rule is recognized over all the earth. (4) Human conduct and society are transformed, so that knowing God changes how people live together. (5) Israel is a light that draws the nations.** Each theme comes with its main verses and a short comment on what they say and why they matter for the essay. The essay's claim, that the prophets see a humanity turned toward the Source of values, rests on all five themes together and not on any single verse.

### 1. Universal Knowledge of God

The most direct way the prophets put it is that **knowledge of God will fill the world.**

> _"They shall not hurt nor destroy in all My holy mountain; for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the Lord, as the waters cover the sea."_ (Isaiah 11:9)

> _"For the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord, as the waters cover the sea."_ (Habakkuk 2:14)

Isaiah and Habakkuk use almost the same picture. The knowledge of God will not belong to a few people. It will be all around us, the way water covers the sea. Isaiah also links this knowledge to the end of violence.

> _"But this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, says the Lord: I will put My law in their inward parts, and in their heart will I write it; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people. And they shall teach no more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying: 'Know the Lord'; for they shall all know Me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them."_ (Jeremiah 31:33-34)

Jeremiah's words are addressed to Israel, so they do not speak about the nations directly. What they add is another side of the vision: **knowledge of God becomes something inward and direct, no longer passed from teacher to student.** This fits the essay's idea of human alignment as a connection to the Source, and not only an agreement on principles. The goal the prophets describe is not a list of agreed ethical rules, but a world in which God is known.

### 2. All Nations Turn to God

The prophets also say that **all peoples, not only Israel, will turn toward God.**

> _"And it shall come to pass in the end of days, that the mountain of the Lord's house shall be established as the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall flow unto it. And many peoples shall go and say: 'Come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord, to the house of the God of Jacob; and He will teach us of His ways, and we will walk in His paths.' For out of Zion shall go forth the Torah, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem."_ (Isaiah 2:2-3; parallel in Micah 4:1-2)

Notice who takes the first step: the nations. They go up to be taught. The text takes for granted that there is something to learn, "His ways" and "the Torah", and this is the picture the essay's section on the Jewish people as a channel depends on. The parallel passage in Micah adds a sober note: _"For all the peoples will walk each one in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the Lord our God for ever and ever"_ (Micah 4:5). Readers understand this in different ways. Some see a stage on the way, others a lasting difference between Israel and the nations. It is worth keeping in mind that **the sources do not all say exactly the same thing.**

> _"For then will I turn to the peoples a pure language, that they may all call upon the name of the Lord, to serve Him with one consent."_ (Zephaniah 3:9)

> _"Thus says the Lord of hosts: It shall yet come to pass, that there shall come peoples, and the inhabitants of many cities; and the inhabitants of one shall go to another, saying: 'Let us go speedily to entreat the favour of the Lord, and to seek the Lord of hosts; I will go also.' Yea, many peoples and mighty nations shall come to seek the Lord of hosts in Jerusalem, and to entreat the favour of the Lord."_ (Zechariah 8:20-22)

Zechariah adds a human touch: people urge one another to go. In the next verse (8:23), ten men from the nations take hold of a Jew and say: _"We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you."_

> _"Also the aliens that join themselves to the Lord, to minister unto Him, and to love the name of the Lord, to be His servants... even them will I bring to My holy mountain, and make them joyful in My house of prayer; their burnt-offerings and their sacrifices shall be acceptable upon My altar; for My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples."_ (Isaiah 56:6-7)

> _"In that day shall Israel be the third with Egypt and with Assyria, even a blessing in the midst of the earth; for that the Lord of hosts has blessed him, saying: 'Blessed be Egypt My people, and Assyria the work of My hands, and Israel My inheritance.'"_ (Isaiah 19:24-25)

Isaiah 19 is striking. Egypt and Assyria, the great powers of the time, are called "My people" and "the work of My hands". The nations are not just included. They are addressed in language that was once used only of Israel.

These verses are about a future in which the nations turn to God. But the tradition also has something to say about the present: the seven commandments given to the descendants of Noah, which apply to all humanity. They forbid idolatry, blasphemy, murder, sexual immorality, theft, and eating a limb torn from a living animal, and they require courts of justice. Maimonides rules that a non-Jew who accepts and keeps them is among the "pious of the nations" and has a share in the World to Come (Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melachim 8:10-11; see ref_2). He adds one condition: **the person must keep them because God commanded them in the Torah, and not because their own reasoning led them there.** Someone who keeps the same laws out of reasoned conviction alone is not counted among the pious of the nations or their wise. (The exact wording and interpretation of this ruling are discussed in later literature.) Two things follow. The tradition has a body of moral law addressed to all humanity, not only to Jews. And it says, in legal form, what the essay argues: the authority of moral values rests on their Source, and not on human reasoning alone.

Taken together, the sources show three things. The return to God is universal, in the prophets' own words. It takes the form of nations coming to learn from a source entrusted to Israel. And the tradition grounds the values addressed to all humanity in that same Source. This is the background to the idea that a body of transmitted teaching could serve humanity as a whole.

### 3. God as King of All the Earth

Another group of verses speaks about God's rule: **one day it will be recognized everywhere.**

> _"And the Lord shall be King over all the earth; on that day the Lord shall be One and His name One."_ (Zechariah 14:9)

> _"And the glory of the Lord shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together; for the mouth of the Lord has spoken it."_ (Isaiah 40:5)

> _"All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn unto the Lord, and all the families of the nations shall worship before You. For the kingdom is the Lord's, and He is the ruler over the nations."_ (Psalms 22:28-29)

Zechariah 14:9 is also the last verse of the *Aleinu* prayer from the beginning of this appendix, where it crowns the hope that all humankind will call on God's name. Its second half says that God and His name will be "One". This is usually understood to mean that everyone will recognize God as the single ultimate reality, so the split between different kinds of worship and different values will end. **The vision is therefore not only about private belief but about authority.** God's rule, and with it the source of values, is acknowledged by the whole world. This is the "vertical dimension" that the essay says is missing from alignment research.

### 4. A Transformed World: From Recognition to Conduct

The prophets connect knowing God with **a change in how people live together.**

> _"And He shall judge between the nations, and shall decide for many peoples; and they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning-hooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more."_ (Isaiah 2:4; parallel in Micah 4:3)

> _"And the wolf shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them."_ (Isaiah 11:6)

Micah continues the picture from Isaiah 2:4 with an image of ordinary security: _"But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid"_ (Micah 4:4). In Isaiah 11:6-9, the peaceful order of the world and "the knowledge of the Lord" in verse 9 belong to one description. **The prophets do not separate belief from conduct.** Knowing God comes with a world rebuilt around justice and peace. This is what the essay means when it says that a return to the Source is a return to values.

### 5. Israel as a Light to the Nations

Finally, the prophets give Israel a role: **to be a light to the nations.**

> _"I the Lord have called you in righteousness, and have taken hold of your hand, and kept you, and set you for a covenant of the people, for a light of the nations."_ (Isaiah 42:6)

> _"It is too light a thing that you should be My servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the offspring of Israel; I will also give you for a light to the nations, that My salvation may be unto the end of the earth."_ (Isaiah 49:6)

> _"Arise, shine, for your light is come, and the glory of the Lord is risen upon you. For, behold, darkness shall cover the earth, and gross darkness the peoples; but upon you the Lord will arise, and His glory shall be seen upon you. And nations shall walk at your light, and kings at the brightness of your rising."_ (Isaiah 60:1-3)

Look at the wording of Isaiah 49:6. Raising up the tribes of Jacob is called "too light a thing". The mission is explicitly bigger than the Jewish people themselves. In Isaiah 60 the nations are drawn by the light, not forced. **The role of the Jewish people as a channel of divine instruction (see "sec_jews_channel") is already stated in the prophets, together with its universal purpose.**
